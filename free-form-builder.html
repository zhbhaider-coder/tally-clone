What a free form builder should include

Most "free" form tools are free only until you actually use them: a 100-response monthly cap, a handful of question types, or a paywall on the one feature you need. Before you commit to a tool, check for these five things:

  • Unlimited forms and submissions. A cap on responses punishes success — the month your form takes off should not be the month it stops working.
  • Enough question types. Text, multiple choice, checkboxes, dropdowns, ratings, dates, and file uploads cover nearly every real-world form, from a contact form to a job application.
  • Conditional logic. Show or hide questions based on earlier answers, so respondents only see what applies to them. A short, relevant form gets far more completions than a long generic one.
  • Easy sharing. A plain link you can paste anywhere, plus an embed option for your own website. If sharing requires an account or a plugin, it slows everything down.
  • A real inbox. Responses should land somewhere you can read, search, and export — not just in your email, mixed in with everything else.

How to build your first form in minutes

You don't need a tutorial to build a form here — the editor works like a document. But if you want a plan, here it is:

  1. Open the form editor and give your form a title, like "Contact us" or "Workshop signup".
  2. Type your first question as a line of text, then pick a question type — short text for a name, email for an address, multiple choice for a preference.
  3. Add the rest of your questions, one per line. Keep it short: every extra question costs you completions. Five to eight questions is a good target for most forms.
  4. Mark the essentials as required — name and email, usually — and leave the rest optional.
  5. Publish and share the link. Anyone with the link can fill the form in; responses appear in your inbox.

Picking the right question types

The question type shapes the answer you'll get. A few rules of thumb:

  • Short text for names, emails, and one-line answers. Long text for feedback, descriptions, and anything open-ended.
  • Multiple choice when there is one right answer (a plan, a date, a preference). Checkboxes when several answers can apply.
  • Ratings (stars or a 1–5 scale) for satisfaction and quick sentiment — easy to answer, easy to average.
  • Dropdowns for long lists like countries or departments, where radio buttons would take over the page.
  • File upload for CVs, photos, or documents — and a signature block when you need consent or agreement.

When in doubt, prefer closed questions (choice, rating) over open text: they take seconds to answer and minutes — not hours — to analyze.

Sharing your form and reading responses

A form only works if people see it. Share the link in an email, a chat message, a social post, or a QR code on a poster — or embed the form directly on your website so visitors never leave the page. After responses come in, open your inbox to read them, and download a CSV when you need the data in a spreadsheet.
